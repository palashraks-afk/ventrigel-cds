    \noindent\normalsize
    \textbf{Background.} The VentriGel first-in-man trial (NCT02305602) treated 15 post-infarction
    patients with an injectable decellularized porcine myocardial matrix and reported that
    improvements in left ventricular remodeling appeared mainly in patients treated more than
    twelve months after infarction. Whether that observation is strong enough to shape a Phase II
    design has not been quantified.
    \textbf{Methods.} No simulated patients were used. The trial's Supplemental Appendix reports
    every endpoint as mean with standard error and sample size, separately for the early
    ($<$12 months) and late ($>$12 months) strata, which determines the standard deviation exactly
    through $\mathrm{SD}=\mathrm{SEM}\sqrt{n}$. The transcription was validated two ways: by
    recomputing all 38 checkable published paired $t$-test $p$-values from the transcribed
    statistics, and by reconstructing each total-cohort mean and standard deviation from its two
    subgroups using the laws of total expectation and total variance. A two-arm Phase II was then
    sized as a function of enrollment enrichment, with the unmeasured control arm, the eligible-pool
    composition, and a winner's-curse discount treated as explicit swept parameters.
    \textbf{Results.} All 38 $p$-values reproduced; the median standard-deviation reconstruction
    error was 3.3\% (0.3\% for end-systolic volume). For LV end-systolic volume the two strata move
    in opposite directions, $+9.3$ mL early against $-7.6$ mL late, so the pooled $-0.35$ mL is a
    cancellation rather than a weak effect. An unselected Phase II therefore has no benefit to
    detect at any sample size, whereas an enriched trial requires 52 randomized patients at face
    value, 92 after a 25\% effect discount, and a bootstrap median of 108 (80\% interval 34--635)
    once the six to eight patients per stratum are taken seriously. Enrichment is not universally
    useful: it is worth roughly twofold for six-minute walk distance, where both strata improve, and
    is counterproductive for ejection fraction, whose pooled signal is produced entirely by the
    early stratum deteriorating.
    \textbf{Conclusions.} The value of enrichment is governed by the sign of the effect in the
    stratum being excluded, not by the size of the pooled effect. Because the source trial was
    single-arm, the early--late contrast cannot be separated from differential natural history;
    under the most conservative attribution the enrichment advantage falls from unbounded to about
    fourfold, while the enriched trial's size is largely unchanged. The robust conclusion is the
    size of the enriched design, not the magnitude of its advantage.

\section{Introduction}

A Phase I trial that establishes safety leaves a sponsor with a design question rather than an
answer: how large must the next trial be? For cardiovascular regenerative therapies this question
is often decisive. Phase II costs scale steeply with the number of patients~\cite{moore2018},
success rates from Phase II onward are low across therapeutic areas~\cite{wong2019}, and sponsors of
single-asset biotechnology companies frequently exhaust their capital between the two phases.

Restricting enrollment to patients more likely to respond --- \emph{enrichment} --- is an
established regulatory strategy for exactly this situation~\cite{fda2019, temple2010}. What is less
often made explicit is that the size of the gain from enrichment varies enormously with the
structure of the heterogeneity, and can be negative.

The usual framing is that broad eligibility criteria ``dilute'' a treatment effect and force larger
sample sizes. That framing is incomplete in a way that matters. Dilution assumes the patients being
added contribute a smaller benefit. If instead they contribute a benefit of the \emph{opposite}
sign, the pooled estimate is not attenuated toward a smaller positive number; it is driven toward
zero, and can cross it. The two situations look identical in a summary table --- both produce a
small pooled effect --- and they have completely different implications for design. In the first,
a larger trial eventually succeeds. In the second, no trial of any size succeeds, because there is
nothing left to detect.

The VentriGel first-in-man trial supplies an unusually clean instance of the second
case~\cite{traverse2019}. Fifteen patients with prior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and
moderate left ventricular dysfunction received transendocardial injections of a hydrogel derived
from decellularized porcine myocardial extracellular
matrix~\cite{singelyn2009, seifnaraghi2013}. The trial was single-arm and explicitly not powered for
efficacy; its stated purpose included informing which patients a later trial should
enroll~\cite{traverse2019}. Post hoc, the investigators reported that improvements in left
ventricular remodeling occurred mainly in patients treated more than twelve months post-infarction.

This paper takes that finding and asks what it implies quantitatively for a Phase II design. The
analysis is deliberately narrow. It does not attempt to predict whether an individual patient will
respond, a claim fifteen single-arm patients cannot support and which is separately dubious on
statistical grounds~\cite{senn2004}. It sizes trials, which is what the published data can bear.

\subsection{Relation to a prior version of this work}

An earlier version of this project, archived at
\href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21516443}{10.5281/zenodo.21516443}, took the opposite approach:
it generated 2{,}000 synthetic patient records from a hand-written eligibility scoring rule, trained
a classifier to predict the resulting label, and reported a test ROC-AUC of 1.00.

That result was circular and the present paper supersedes it. The target variable in that design was
a deterministic threshold function of the same features supplied to the classifier, so the model was
recovering a conditional statement its author had written; near-perfect accuracy was the arithmetic
consequence of the construction rather than evidence about biology. Reported feature importances
likewise reproduced the penalty weights chosen by hand. The classifier code is retained in the
repository under \texttt{deprecated/} so the comparison can be inspected. This correction is stated
plainly here because the erroneous version carries a DOI and may be encountered independently.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Recovering the variance structure}

The trial's Supplemental Appendix reports each endpoint as a mean with its standard error and the
contributing $n$, given separately for the total cohort and for the early ($<$12 months) and late
($>$12 months) strata. Because $\mathrm{SEM}=\mathrm{SD}/\sqrt{n}$, the sample standard deviation
follows exactly:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SD} = \mathrm{SEM}\sqrt{n}.
\end{equation}
This supplies everything a power calculation requires. No quantity in this study was digitized from
a figure, imputed, or simulated. Nine endpoints were transcribed with their source table recorded
alongside each value.

\subsection{Validating the transcription}

Two independent checks were applied before any of the recovered numbers were used.

\textbf{Check 1: reproducing the published $p$-values.} The trial compared each follow-up visit
against baseline with a paired Student's $t$-test. Given the mean and SEM of the paired change, the
test statistic is $t=\text{mean}/\mathrm{SEM}$ on $n-1$ degrees of freedom. Recomputing every
published $p$-value from the transcribed statistics simultaneously tests the transcription, the
identity in Equation~1, and the stated test.

\textbf{Check 2: reconstructing the pooled cohort.} The total cohort is the union of the two strata,
so its moments must follow from theirs by the laws of total expectation and total variance:
\begin{align}
\mu &= \textstyle\sum_g w_g\,\mu_g,\\
\sigma^2 &= \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_g w_g\,\sigma_g^2}_{\text{within}}
          + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_g w_g\,(\mu_g-\mu)^2}_{\text{between}} .
\end{align}
The published total-cohort values were never used as inputs, so agreement is a genuine test. It is a
demanding one, because the between-stratum term is driven entirely by the subgroup separation that
the rest of the analysis depends on.

\subsection{Sizing the trial as a function of enrichment}

Let $\pi$ denote the fraction of the \emph{eligible pool} belonging to the late stratum, and $e$ the
fraction of \emph{enrolled} patients drawn from it. Setting $e=\pi$ describes an unselected trial and
$e=1$ a fully enriched one. For a treatment-arm change $d_g$ and an assumed control-arm change $c_g$
in stratum $g$, the estimand of a two-arm trial is
\begin{equation}
\Delta(e) = \textstyle\sum_g w_g(e)\,(d_g - c_g), \qquad w_{\text{late}}(e)=e,
\end{equation}
with the variance of the enrolled population given by Equation~3 evaluated at $w_g(e)$. Required
sample size scales as $\sigma^2/\Delta^2$, so enrichment acts on both terms at once: it raises
$|\Delta|$ by removing patients who dilute or oppose the effect, and lowers $\sigma$ by eliminating
between-stratum heterogeneity. Per-arm sample size was solved against the noncentral $t$
distribution rather than the normal approximation, because the enriched designs of interest are
small enough for the difference to matter. All designs use $\alpha=0.05$ two-sided, 80\% power, and
10\% dropout before the primary assessment; the Phase I lost one of fifteen patients to
cardiac magnetic resonance follow-up.

A design whose effect points away from benefit still returns a finite sample size, because $n$
depends on $\Delta^2$. Such an $n$ is the size required to detect a difference of that magnitude in
either direction, which is not the same as a trial capable of showing the therapy works. Every
design reported below is flagged for the sign of its effect, and cost optimization is restricted to
designs that favor treatment.

\subsection{The screening penalty}

Enrichment is not free. To randomize $N$ patients at enrichment level $e$ from a pool of composition
$\pi$, the number who must be assessed for eligibility is
\begin{equation}
S = \max\!\left(\frac{eN}{\pi},\; \frac{(1-e)N}{1-\pi}\right),
\end{equation}
whichever stratum is exhausted first. A cost model separates per-screen from per-patient costs and
derives enrollment duration from screening throughput, since screening is the rate limit in an
enriched trial. Unit costs are stated as planning assumptions rather than measurements; sample-size
ratios, which are the paper's primary claims, do not depend on them.

\subsection{The assumptions that are swept rather than asserted}

\textbf{The control arm.} This is the central limitation of the analysis and it is treated as a
parameter throughout rather than as a closing caveat. The Phase I was single-arm: it reports what
happened to treated patients, not what would have happened untreated. The early--late contrast is
therefore consistent with two different stories. Under effect modification, the therapy genuinely
works better late and the early stratum's adverse volume change reflects a real absence of benefit.
Under differential natural history, untreated early patients continue to dilate over six months
while untreated late patients are stable, so part of the observed gap is ordinary post-infarct
remodeling. These are not distinguishable from single-arm data. Both stratum-specific control
changes were therefore gridded over their full plausible ranges, including the maximally
conservative case in which the entire early-stratum change is attributed to natural history.

\textbf{The winner's curse.} The late stratum was identified post hoc in the same fifteen patients
that produced its effect estimate, which guarantees upward bias: the subgroup was selected partly
because its noise pointed favorably~\cite{ioannidis2008, wang2007, sun2010}. Headline results
therefore apply a 25\% discount to the published stratum effects, and the discount is swept from 40\%
to none.

\textbf{Estimation uncertainty.} The stratum estimates rest on six to eight patients. A parametric
bootstrap draws each stratum's true mean and variance from their sampling distributions,
$\mu\sim\bar{x}+(s/\sqrt{n})\,t_{n-1}$ and $\sigma^2\sim(n-1)s^2/\chi^2_{n-1}$, and re-solves the
entire design on each of 4{,}000 draws. Draws in which the effect reverses sign are retained as
infeasible rather than discarded, since discarding them would bias the interval optimistically.

Simulation appears in this study only in this subsection, and only to propagate uncertainty already
present in the published estimates or to map the range of assumptions under which a conclusion
holds. It is never used to create observations.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The transcription is faithful}

All 38 checkable published $p$-values were reproduced from the transcribed mean, SEM and $n$ at the
precision the source prints. Four cells were excluded for documented reasons rather than counted as
failures. Three are B-type natriuretic peptide, whose table reports percent change but whose
$t$-test column tests absolute values; the published $p=0.63$ corresponds to the absolute change of
$+17.2$ pg/mL with a paired SEM near 35 pg/mL, which is consistent with a 294.8 pg/mL baseline. The
fourth is total-cohort scar fraction, where the printed $0.50\,(1.9)$ at $n=12$ implies $p=0.80$
rather than the printed $p=0.58$; this is an unresolved discrepancy in the source, recorded and not
corrected, and scar fraction is not used in any headline result.

Reconstructing each pooled cohort from its two strata gave a median standard-deviation error of
3.3\%. For LV end-systolic volume the reconstruction returned a mean of $-0.36$ mL against a
published $-0.35$ mL, and a standard deviation of 14.26 mL against a published 14.22 mL, an error of
0.3\%. Since the published totals were never inputs, this agreement establishes that the recovered
stratum variances are correct and, in particular, that the between-stratum separation is real
arithmetic rather than an artifact of transcription.

\subsection{The pooled effect is a cancellation}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../results/figures/fig1_subgroup_cancellation.pdf}
\caption{Six-month change from baseline by treatment timing. Bars are published means, whiskers
published standard errors, signs flipped so that right is benefit. For end-systolic volume, viable
mass and quality of life the strata move in opposite directions, so the pooled bar is the residue of
two offsetting effects rather than a small common one.}
\label{fig:cancel}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:cancel} shows the six-month changes. For LV end-systolic volume the early stratum
worsened by $9.3$ mL while the late stratum improved by $7.6$ mL, giving a pooled change of
$-0.35$ mL. The pooled figure is not a weak treatment effect. It is two effects of comparable
magnitude and opposite sign averaging to approximately nothing. The same pattern holds for viable
myocardial mass ($-10.5$ g early, $+15.0$ g late) and, less symmetrically, for the Minnesota Living
with Heart Failure score. Ejection fraction shows the mirror image: the early stratum declined
significantly ($-3.8$\%, $p=0.03$) while the late stratum did not ($-0.6$\%, $p=0.69$), so the
pooled decline is produced entirely by one stratum.

Table~\ref{tab:n} gives the central result. For end-systolic volume the unselected pooled effect
points marginally toward harm, so no sample size demonstrates benefit; the enriched trial requires
52 patients. Applying the 25\% winner's-curse discount raises this to 92, and the bootstrap median
is 108 with an 80\% interval of 34 to 635, with 98\% of draws favoring treatment.

Figure~\ref{fig:curves} decomposes why enrichment works so strongly here. Moving from $e=\pi$ to
$e=1$ raises the effect from $-0.29$ to $+7.6$ mL while simultaneously lowering the standard
deviation from 14.3 to 9.1 mL, because the between-stratum component of variance vanishes when only
one stratum is enrolled. The two gains compound in the ratio $\sigma^2/\Delta^2$.

\subsection{Enrichment is not universally beneficial}

The most useful result is the heterogeneity across endpoints. For six-minute walk distance both
strata improve ($+40.5$ m early, $+50.9$ m late), so enrichment buys only a factor of about 1.6, and
an unselected trial of 50 patients is already feasible. For ejection fraction, enrichment is
actively counterproductive: the pooled signal exists only because the early stratum deteriorated, so
restricting enrollment to late patients removes it. That signal is a possible safety observation
rather than an efficacy one, which makes its removal a governance question and not merely a
statistical inconvenience.

The governing quantity is therefore the sign of the effect in the stratum being excluded, not the
magnitude of the pooled effect. When excluded patients contribute a negative effect, full enrichment
is always statistically optimal and the pooled estimate can be worthless at any $N$. When they
contribute a positive but smaller effect, enrichment yields a modest gain and a genuine interior
optimum appears once screening costs are counted.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the missing control arm}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../results/figures/fig4_control_drift.pdf}
\caption{Enrichment advantage across every combination of assumed control-arm drift, with a 25\%
effect discount applied. The circled point is the maximally conservative attribution, in which the
entire early-stratum volume change is natural history rather than absence of benefit.}
\label{fig:drift}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:drift} maps the advantage over the full space of control-arm assumptions. The
enriched design's own size is unaffected by early-stratum drift, since it enrolls no early patients;
what the assumption changes is the comparator, and therefore the size of the claimed advantage. At
the maximally conservative point, where the early stratum's entire $+9.3$ mL is attributed to
natural history, the unselected trial becomes solvable at 446 patients and the advantage falls from
unbounded to roughly ninefold at face value, or about fourfold after the discount. The enriched
trial remains near 52 patients throughout.

This is the honest summary: the \emph{size of the enriched design} is robust, while the \emph{size
of its advantage} is not, because the latter depends on a quantity the Phase I could not measure.

Six-minute walk distance is more fragile still, and in the opposite direction. Its apparent
feasibility rests on assuming no control-arm change, which is implausible for a test with known
practice and placebo effects in heart failure populations. A control-arm improvement of 30 m raises
the unselected requirement from 50 to 390 patients and the enriched requirement from 32 to 178. The
endpoint that looks cheapest is the one most exposed to the missing control arm.

\subsection{Uncertainty and economics}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../results/figures/fig6_design_summary.pdf}
\caption{Required patients per candidate primary endpoint. Squares are unselected point estimates;
right-pointing markers indicate endpoints with no pooled benefit to detect at any size. Circles and
bars are enriched bootstrap medians with 80\% intervals, after a 25\% effect discount.}
\label{fig:summary}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:summary} combines the discount and the bootstrap into one view. Intervals are wide
--- the enriched end-systolic volume design spans 34 to 635 patients --- which is the appropriate
representation of what six to eight patients per stratum can support. Reporting 52 without that
interval would overstate the precision available.

Under the stated cost assumptions, the enriched end-systolic volume trial costs approximately
\$7.7M at face value or \$11.6M after the discount, over 31 to 39 months. The screening multiplier
at $\pi=0.53$ is only 1.9, so screening is not binding. It becomes binding as the eligible pool
thins: at $\pi=0.03$ the trial is still 52 patients but requires 1{,}733 screens and over three
decades of enrollment at six sites. Where responders are rare, calendar time constrains the design
before sample size does, which an analysis modeling enrollment alone would miss.

\section{Discussion}

The practical recommendation that follows is specific. A Phase II of VentriGel powered on left
ventricular end-systolic volume should restrict enrollment to patients more than twelve months
post-infarction and plan for roughly 90 to 110 randomized patients, with the understanding that the
upper end of the plausible range extends beyond 600. An unselected trial on the same endpoint should
not be run at all, because the pooled estimate the Phase I supports is indistinguishable from zero
and may point the wrong way. If instead a sponsor wants the cheapest possible signal-seeking trial,
six-minute walk distance unselected at roughly 50 patients is the least expensive design in
Table~\ref{tab:n} --- but that figure assumes a control arm with no improvement, and the estimate
degrades faster than any other in the table once that assumption is relaxed.

More generally, the analysis suggests that the common description of heterogeneity as ``dilution''
obscures the decision that matters. Dilution implies a recoverable effect and a larger trial.
Cancellation implies no recoverable effect and no viable trial. Distinguishing the two requires
subgroup-level effect estimates, which are frequently published, and requires reading a small pooled
mean as a question rather than as an answer.

\subsection{Limitations}

The single-arm design of the source trial is the dominant limitation and is the reason control drift
is a swept parameter rather than a fixed assumption. Nothing in this analysis can establish that
VentriGel works better in late patients; it establishes what follows for trial design \emph{if} the
reported pattern is real.

The late stratum was defined post hoc, and the fifteen-patient cohort supplies six to eight patients
per stratum for the imaging endpoints. The discount and bootstrap address this but cannot eliminate
it. Cardiac magnetic resonance follow-up was incomplete, so stratum $n$ differs across endpoints and
weights were assigned by the $n$ actually contributing at each visit.

The prevalence of late-stratum patients in a real eligible pool is unknown. The trial enrolled the
two strata deliberately balanced, so its 8/15 split estimates its own design, not the population;
this is why prevalence is swept rather than fixed. Unit costs are planning assumptions.

Finally, statistical detectability is not clinical importance. A 7.6 mL reduction on a 142.3 mL
baseline is a 5.3\% change, which is modest against the reverse-remodeling thresholds usually
considered clinically meaningful. A trial powered for the effect estimated here would establish that
the effect exists, not that it is worth having. Setting a clinically motivated target rather than
the observed effect would increase every sample size in Table~\ref{tab:n}.

\subsection{What would settle it}

The analysis identifies exactly one measurement that would collapse most of the remaining
uncertainty: the six-month change in end-systolic volume among untreated patients matched on time
since infarction. That quantity is the horizontal axis of Figure~\ref{fig:drift}, and it is
obtainable from existing observational cohorts with serial cardiac magnetic resonance without any
new trial. Supplying it would convert the enrichment advantage from a range spanning an order of
magnitude into a point estimate.

\section{Reproducibility}

All results are generated by \texttt{python run\_analysis.py} and \texttt{python make\_figures.py}
in under a minute, with no arguments and no downloaded data. Published values are transcribed in
\texttt{ventrigel/trial\_data.py} with the source table recorded for each. The validation of
Section~3.1 runs on every execution, so a transcription error would surface as a failed check rather
than as a silently wrong result.
