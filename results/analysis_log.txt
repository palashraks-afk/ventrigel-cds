[3/6]  Sample size, unselected versus enriched
==============================================================================
  Two-arm parallel design, alpha=0.05, power=80%, 10% dropout, noncentral t.
  Eligible-pool late fraction pi=0.533. No control drift and no
  shrinkage applied here; both are imposed in step 5.

  endpoint        eff unsel      N unsel  eff enr   N enrich      d     ratio
  lvesv               -0.29       88,708     7.60         52   0.84    1,706x  <- wrong sign
  six_min_walk        46.05           50    50.90         32   1.09        2x
  viable_mass          3.10        2,942    15.00         42   0.96       70x
  ef                  -2.09          138    -0.60      1,748  -0.14     worse  <- wrong sign
  lvedv                4.61          730     7.60        208   0.41        4x
  mlwhfq               7.73          540    15.10        138   0.51        4x

  Effects are signed so that positive means benefit. Sample size depends
  on the square of the effect, so a design whose effect points the wrong
  way still returns a finite n; that n is the size needed to detect a
  difference of that magnitude in either direction, not the cost of
  demonstrating benefit. The flagged rows have no benefit to detect.

  Enrichment is not uniformly good. It is decisive for LVESV and viable
  mass, where the strata oppose; worth about a factor of two for the
  6-min walk, where both strata improve; and counterproductive for
  ejection fraction, whose pooled signal is produced entirely by the
  early stratum worsening -- a safety signal enrichment would erase.

[5/6]  Structural assumptions: control drift and winner's curse
==============================================================================
  The Phase I was single-arm, so control-arm drift is unmeasured. If
  untreated early patients dilate on their own, part of the early-versus-
  late gap is natural history rather than effect modification.

  Enriched N for lvesv depends only on the late stratum, so it is
  constant in the early-arm drift. What the drift changes is the
  *unselected* comparator, and so the size of the advantage. Grid of

[6/6]  Cost, and why maximal enrichment is not optimal
==============================================================================
  Unit costs (assumptions, not measurements): fixed $2,000,000; $65,000/patient; $2,500/screen; $12,000/site-month; 6 sites at 0.75/site/month

  Screening cost rises as 1/pi for a fully enriched trial, so the cheapest
  design generally sits below full enrichment.

  lvesv:
    unselected (e=0.53): pooled effect points toward harm (-0.29); no benefit to power for
    cheapest   (e=1.00): N=52, screened=98, $7.7M, 31 months
    full       (e=1.00): N=52, screened=98, $7.7M
  viable_mass:
    unselected (e=0.53): N=2,942, $240.9M
    cheapest   (e=1.00): N=42, screened=79, $6.7M, 26 months
    full       (e=1.00): N=42, screened=79, $6.7M

  Whether an interior optimum exists depends on the sign of the effect in
  the stratum being excluded, and the two candidate endpoints differ:
    lvesv         excluded stratum has a NEGATIVE effect (+9.3 mL, i.e. harm)
    six_min_walk  excluded stratum has a POSITIVE effect (+40.5 m, smaller)
  Sweeping the eligible-pool composition makes the difference visible.

  For LVESV the optimum never leaves full enrichment, because every early
  patient admitted carries an effect of the wrong sign and strictly reduces
  the pooled effect. The screening penalty still exists; it just appears as
  duration rather than sample size, and duration is what actually kills the
  design. At a 3% eligible-pool prevalence the enriched trial is still only
  52 patients but takes over three decades to enroll at six sites.

  For the 6-min walk, where excluded patients still improve, the optimum is
  interior and moves off the boundary as responders get scarce. An analysis
  that models enrolment but not screening would miss both behaviours and
  conclude that tighter criteria are always better.
